TOPIC: How Was The First Computer Invented? SCRIPT: It is 1943. You sit at a long desk, pencil behind your ear. Crank. Clunk. Crank. Clunk. One number done. Your name is Mia. Your job title? Computer. Not a machine... you. Mrs. Hart leans over your shoulder. Check it twice, Mia. Always twice. You sigh. You always check it twice. And your lunch... is still cold. Again. Back then, a computer was a person. The other kind didn't exist. The army needed firing tables for its big guns. Each table needed thousands of flight paths. One path, thirty seconds in the air, took a person about twenty hours. Twenty hours! Then Major Cole walks in. A professor wants to build a machine, he says. Eighteen thousand glass tubes. Nothing to crank. Everyone laughs. You don't. Here's the rule. A machine can't guess. It does exactly what it's told... nothing more. Tell it wrong, and it answers wrong. Perfectly. So how was the first computer invented? And what did it cost? That night, Mrs. Hart tells you the real story. In 1837, Charles Babbage designed a machine of brass gears. Punched cards, borrowed from weaving looms, would run it. Did he finish it? No. The money ran out. But the design had a store for numbers and a mill to crunch them. Sound familiar? That's a memory and a processor. Then, in 1843, Ada Lovelace wrote a method for it to work out Bernoulli numbers. Many call it the first program. A program... for a machine that never existed. Then, in 1936, Alan Turing pictured one machine that could do any calculation... if given the right steps. Not a gadget. An idea. Then ideas met metal. In 1941, in Berlin, Konrad Zuse switched on the Z3. Thousands of clicking relays. Programmable. Brilliant. And hardly anyone knew. In Britain, Colossus cracked coded messages with glowing tubes. Its existence stayed secret for decades. Genius... with no credit. Then you see it. Philadelphia. ENIAC. Sam, a young engineer, pats it like a pet. Meet the beast, he says. Thirty tons. About one hundred and seventy square meters of cabinets. Eighteen thousand glass tubes. How fast is it? you ask. Sam grins. About five thousand additions every second. You add one every few seconds. Not even close. Plus: speed. Minus: no keyboard. No screen. So how do you give it orders? You rewire it. Cables. Switches. Days of work for one problem. Guess who gets the job? You. And five other women. Plug. Check. Plug. Check. Twice. The catch? Tubes burn out. One dead tube among eighteen thousand... and everything stops. So the engineers barely switch it off. The room gets hot as an oven. Is this the future? Or a very expensive heater? And there's a quieter cost. Down the hall, the room of human computers shrinks. Fewer every week. Crank. Clunk. Silence. Is that progress... or loss? Both. Same day. Mrs. Hart packs her desk on a Friday. Her calculator goes to storage. She hands you her pencil. Mia, a machine is only as good as the person who tells it what to do. Check it twice. Always twice. Your throat tightens. She taught you everything. And now she's been replaced by cables. She smiles anyway. You hold the pencil... tight. February 1946. Demo day. Generals. Reporters. Flashbulbs. Major Cole nods at you. Start it. Lights ripple across the cabinets. A shell flies for thirty seconds. By hand, that's twenty hours. Seconds later, the printout clicks out. The shell lands... underground. A hush. Cole whispers. Let it go. Nobody can check it. Nobody... except you. Because you wired it. You remember midnight. One cable. Maybe one socket off. A wrong table means real guns miss real targets. And the machine will never doubt itself. Who would ever know? Would you? Hundreds of eyes. Ten seconds. Your job. Cole's career. The applause... or the truth? You step forward. Hands shaking. That was my mistake, you say. One cable. Wrong socket. Gasps. You swap it. Start again. Thirty seconds. The printout clicks. This time, the shell lands exactly where it should. Silence. Then applause. Cole exhales. A reporter shouts. How did you know? Check it twice, you say. A machine only does what you tell it. So tell it the truth. In the back row, Mrs. Hart, in her good hat, grins. That evening, you finally eat lunch... hot. Late. But hot. Is any of this real? ENIAC was. It was unveiled in 1946 at the University of Pennsylvania, built by John Mauchly and J. Presper Eckert. Six women programmed it, including Kay McNulty and Betty Jennings. Their names faded... for decades. Mia is fiction. And the title first computer is slippery. Babbage designed one. Zuse built one. Colossus and ENIAC used tubes. But a machine that stores its own instructions came later. So which one deserves the crown? Two years later, in a cold lab in Manchester, a tiny machine hums. It holds its own instructions in memory. And it just ran its first program. What do you think? Tell us in the comments, and tell us which invention story we should do next.
